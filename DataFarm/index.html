What DataFarm adds to the video model

Structure from the simulator, appearance from the real cameras. Edge maps of the untextured simulator video fix arm pose, object positions, and timing; real reference frames and a picture of the robot supply only the look.

All cameras in one generation. Head and wrist views are tiled on one canvas and generated as one video, so they share lighting and materials and agree on which objects exist and what each gripper holds.

References and prompts that add no content, and long episodes without drift: references match the simulated task state, prompts describe only the current segment, and long episodes are generated as independent chunks and joined where neighbouring chunks differ least.

The problem

Robot data is still collected one episode at a time

  • Each episode needs a robot, an operator, and a reset.
  • Every new task, layout, or scene starts the collection over.
  • The cost grows with every episode you record.
DataFarm

A coding agent takes the teleoperator's place

It does not drive the robot: it compiles the task into a simulation and a render contract, and the farm produces every further episode.

01Calibrate

One calibration episode: a short recording of the robot in the target scene, plus a request such as "100 episodes of this task". None of its actions are reused.

02Simulate

The agent fits cameras and scene, qualifies the simulator, and writes an expert against a judge it cannot see; the simulator fixes actions, states, and contacts.

03Render

A video model conditioned on real frames renders every rollout as photographic video, all cameras in one generation.

admitted
04Admit

Machine checks and an independent reviewer compare every render with its simulated state; only episodes that agree enter the dataset.

Rendering with real images is what transfers. Sim + DR shares the grounding, the expert, and the actions with DataFarm and differs only in how the frames are produced. The farm varies surfaces and lighting around fixed, exact trajectories, so the policy cannot rely on how the table looks.

The paper

Certification is kept apart from production

Because agents write both the expert and its checks, and the video model may alter the scene, every stage is checked by a component other than its producer.

  1. Grounding and simulator qualification. The agent fits the cameras and metric scale, estimates object geometry and articulation, and labels each quantity as measured, fitted, assumed, or unknown; short probes check that the simulator can reproduce the skills the task requires.
  2. Task contract and expert. The expert is developed against a judge it cannot inspect and admitted only on held-out initial states; every admitted trajectory is one continuous execution.
  3. Rendering with real images. The renderer may alter appearance but not geometry, objects, or timing; all cameras are generated together from the simulator's structure.
  4. Verification and admission. Every rendered episode is compared with its simulated state by machine checks and an independent reviewer; all attempts and failures are kept.

Abstract

Teleoperation remains the default way to collect robot demonstrations, but every episode costs robot and operator time, and every new task starts from scratch. We ask whether a coding agent can take over the teleoperator's job. In our DataFarm, the agent receives a data request and one calibration episode of the robot in the target scene and rebuilds the task in simulation: it writes the scene, an expert controller, and a success judge. The simulator supplies exact actions, and a video model conditioned on real images renders each rollout as photographic video, generating all camera views together consistently. The output is real-ready data: synchronized camera views labelled with the robot's actions, on which a policy can be trained directly and deployed on the real robot. Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission. On five real-robot tasks, policies trained only on farm data match policies trained on as many teleoperated episodes (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials), and under an unseen tablecloth and coloured light they keep 87 successes where the teleoperation-trained policies fall to 46. Adapting a task is fully automatic and costs $21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, plus $5 for a render contract; every further episode costs $0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, and review, with no robot or operator. One hour of three-view data for a task costs $107 on average, these costs included, slightly below a quote of $117 for teleoperation in China and below $290 in labour alone in North America.
